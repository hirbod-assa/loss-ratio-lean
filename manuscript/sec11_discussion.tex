\section{Discussion and open problems}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The standard.}
The proposed standard asks authors of AI-heavy work for information that a reviewer can use, in proportion to how
heavily AI was used, and it separates what can be verified from what must be declared. Several questions are open.
Where exactly the boundaries between the tiers lie will become clear only from use. Whether the verification of
formal statements against the paper can be shared between author, reviewer and the community is a question for
journals. The standard also presupposes that formal verification is feasible for
part of the work; for risk management this depends on libraries of formalized probability and Choquet integration
that do not yet exist. Finally, the comparison with large-scale efforts suggests that the design principles of
multi-agent work, and the records needed to report it, are largely independent of scale, but this is based on one
case study.

The case study itself bears on the standard in four ways. The errors were found by different checks, and a model of
another provider found three that all earlier checks had missed (Table~\ref{tab:errors}); reporting the checks, not
only their conclusion, tells the reader which kinds of error were looked for. The decisive steps came from the
author's objections, to an AI-drafted formulation that assumed what should have been proved and to axioms that used
derived quantities; a record of the division of labour makes such contributions visible. The revisions after the
Lean check changed the axioms, so that the formal check covers an equivalent earlier formulation; a verification
status stated part by part, with dates, makes this visible. And agreement among AI reviews does not establish
correctness, nor does a formal check cover more than was formalized.

\paragraph{A method for AI-assisted proofs.}
The broader lesson the author draws from the case study is that the use of AI in mathematical proofs should become
methodological. It should be a procedure that is written down, tested, improved and reused by others, rather than
each researcher's private way of prompting a chatbot. This paper introduces one such method. It combines the
author's background in mathematics with the practices of AI-assisted coding that have become common: version
control, agents with defined roles, adversarial checks, and records kept as files. The repository of the paper
contains a skill file that describes the method and a set of agent definitions that others can run directly, tested
on a small example problem (Section~\ref{sec:std-use}). Whether the method carries over to other problems and other
authors can only be learned from use, and the skill and the agents are offered so that this can be tried.

\paragraph{The research community.}
There is a concern in the actuarial research community, which the author shares, that AI will diminish the credit
that comes from research. If agents can propose, prove and check results of the kind reported here within days, the
signal carried by such a result weakens. The concern is sharper for a community of the size of actuarial science, in
which research, education and the profession are closely linked: doctoral students learn by proving, and if the
proving is delegated it is not clear how that ability is acquired or shown; and access to agents at scale is unequal,
so that credit could come to follow computing resources rather than ideas. The case study suggests two partial
answers. Its decisive contributions were the choice of the question and the objections to AI-drafted formulations, so
credit may move from producing proofs to posing questions, judging formulations and taking responsibility for what is
certified; and a report of the kind proposed here makes the division of labour visible (item~2), which is a
precondition for assigning credit fairly. Whether journals, hiring committees and the profession adopt such signals,
and how the training of young researchers should change, are questions for the community as a whole, and they
deserve discussion before practice settles them by default.

\paragraph{The case study.}
A loss ratio carries its own premium and its own risk measure. The premium is the price point of the cash line,
the sure payment at which the book changes hands, and the risk measure is loss ratio times premium, visible even
where the ratio explodes as the residue of the singularity. With this reading, axioms on the values of the loss
ratio alone, whose substance is that the underwriting results of comonotone books of zero premium add, together with
law invariance and regularity of two sure amounts, characterize the loss ratios that are ratios of two Choquet-type
functionals. The characterization is sharp, and the pair is unique. At the level of representations it is a
transfer of the theory of \citet{yaari1987}, \citet{schmeidler1986}, \citet{wyp1997} and \citet{www2020}; at the
level of the loss ratio it is new, and it comes with facts that have no single-functional counterpart: no non-trivial
loss ratio is monotone (Proposition~\ref{prop:F2} in the Supplement), the association with a family of risk measures holds exactly
off $\{\rho=\Pi=0\}$ and is signed (Corollary~\ref{cor:assoc} in the Supplement), and no axiom on finite values of
the ratio can replace the pooling axiom (Proposition~\ref{prop:nofinite}).

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{Beyond comonotone pooling.} The most promising direction is to drop \axR{} in favour of an axiom
  that allows diversification, so that the implied premium and risk measure are convex or subadditive rather
  than comonotonic additive. The implied premium and the implied risk measure are defined without \axR{} only
  partly (the premium needs only \axP; the accounting reading of $\rho$ needs pooling with sure losses), and the
  resulting class, a ratio of Kusuoka-type functionals, has no characterization yet. Replacing comonotonic
  additivity by convexity leads to suprema of signed Choquet integrals \citep{liu2020}, so no single pair $(h,g)$
  and no uniqueness should be expected.
\item \emph{Weaker cardinal axioms.} Can \axR{} be replaced by the mediant inequality plus a supplementary
  condition? By Section~\ref{sec:necessity}, such a condition must involve non-finite values of $\LR$. The inequality implies additivity for comonotone pairs strictly on the same side of break-even, but
  not for pairs straddling $1$ or lying in the break-even set.
\item \emph{Order properties of $\LR$.} \axRM{} is an order property of $\LR$, but of the sure amounts at which
  its cash lines become non-finite or vanish. Whether it is equivalent to an order property of the values of $\LR$ alone is open; by
  Proposition~\ref{prop:F2} it cannot be pointwise monotonicity.
\end{enumerate}
Further open problems, on loadings on sure losses, quasi-convexity, the actuarial domain and unbounded losses, are
listed in Section~\ref{supp:open} of the Supplement.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has proposed a standard for reporting AI-assisted proofs in mathematical risk management and actuarial
science, with nine items applied in three tiers according to how heavily AI was used, and it has applied the
standard in full to a case study: the characterization of the loss ratio by axioms on its values, obtained with a
panel of AI agents under the author's direction. The broader lesson is that the use of AI in mathematical proofs
should become methodological, written down, tested, improved and reused, combining a background in mathematics with
the practices of AI-assisted coding: version control, agents with defined roles, adversarial checks, and records kept
as files. The standard, the skill file that describes the
method and the agent definitions that others can run directly, tested on a small example problem, are offered as a
first version for the community to test and improve.
