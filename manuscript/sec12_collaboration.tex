\section{The case study: report of the AI process}\label{sec:collab}

This section reports how the result of Sections~\ref{sec:lit}--\ref{sec:necessity} was obtained with AI, following
the items of the standard in Table~\ref{tab:standard}; the case study is in tier~(iii). The tools are identified in
the declaration before the references (item~1). The section describes the organization of the work (items~2
and~3), the knowledge base (item~4), the errors found (item~5), the guardrails (item~6), the formal check in Lean
(item~7), the revisions prompted by readers, and the verification status (item~8). The records (item~9) are in the
repository named in the code availability statement.

\subsection{Organization of the work}\label{sec:collab-org}

The work used Claude (Anthropic) through the Claude desktop application in Cowork mode, which runs the assistant in
a sandboxed environment and can coordinate several AI agents in a workflow; the later revisions, including the
second panel of Section~\ref{sec:collab-revision}, used Claude Code in the author's code editor, which ran the panel
as a scripted workflow. AI agents worked on designated parts of
the project, under the author's direction. A main assistant organized the work and drafted and revised the
manuscript. A roundtable of six agents, five of them each assigned a strand of the literature and one acting as
chair, surveyed the axiomatic theory of premiums, risk measures and performance ratios and produced a synthesis. A
larger panel of agents covered ten literature strands, proposed candidate statements, ranked them by means of judge
agents, drafted proofs, and subjected the proofs to separate AI-assisted checking runs over several rounds. The
literature review was conducted by these AI assistants under the author's supervision: the author defined the
strands to be covered and the questions to be answered, and selected and framed the material presented in
Section~\ref{sec:lit}. Some agents were given the names of researchers as personas, to represent the
perspective of a literature strand; these were simulations, and the researchers concerned did not participate, were
not consulted and bear no responsibility for the content. The research questions, the choice of formulation and all
decisions on the content of the paper were made by the author.

the author assigns the task of each AI component and reviews its output. Figure~\ref{fig:panel} shows the internal pipeline of the panel, in which candidate statements and
proofs pass through ranking and repeated critical review before being returned to the author.
Figure~\ref{fig:panelpic} gives a schematic picture of the panel: the ten strand agents around a shared digest,

\subsection{How the knowledge base for this paper was assembled}\label{sec:collab-kb}

The agents draw on two kinds of knowledge: what their underlying models learned in training, which can be
recalled only approximately, and documents read into their working context during the task. In this project the
second kind was organized as follows.

The five strands of the roundtable were identified by the author: premium principles and capital allocation;
conic finance and performance ratios; coherent risk measures and acceptance sets; axiomatic risk measures and
elicitability; and the extended-loss-ratio framework with its association. Its synthesis served as the starting
point of the panel.

The panel divided the literature into ten strands: insurance-pricing axiomatics; Choquet integrals and comonotonic
additivity; signed Choquet integrals; law-invariant coherent and convex risk measures; acceptability indices and
quasi-convex duality; return and star-shaped risk measures; elicitability; performance-ratio axiomatics; decision
theory and functional equations; and a critique of the earlier synthesis. Each strand agent returned exact
statements of if-and-only-if theorems with their hypotheses, a confidence label (high, medium or low) and a note
on its source. For example, the signed representation results of
\citet{cmmm2012,cmmm2015} were taken from their working paper versions.

There was no separate retrieval index. Instead, the strand reports were merged into a written digest of about
415{,}000 characters, which, together with the ten candidate formulations (about 245{,}000 characters), formed the
shared knowledge base of the later stages: the judge and prover agents were instructed to read these files in full
before working, and Section~\ref{sec:lit} was written from the digest. The confidence labels therefore
travelled with each statement into the stages that used it.

In addition, the author used Gemini (Google) to prepare summaries of the key works cited in
Section~\ref{sec:lit}: \citet{gerber1974}, \citet{yaari1987} and \citet{wyp1997} on premium principles;
\citet{msm1994}, \citet{cmmm2012,cmmm2015} and \citet{www2020} on signed Choquet integrals; \citet{adeh1999} and
\citet{kusuoka2001} on coherent risk measures; and \citet{chernymadan2009}, \citet{chew1983} and \citet{dekel1986} on
performance ratios and ratio representations. Each summary stated the core results of the work and their role in
this paper. The descriptions in Section~\ref{sec:lit} and the uses of these works in Sections~\ref{sec:axioms},
\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:necessity} and~\ref{sec:consequences} were compared with these summaries; no
discrepancy was found, and three descriptions in Section~\ref{sec:lit} were made more precise. Since the summaries
are themselves AI output, this comparison is a consistency check, not a check against the original publications.

Later, the author supplied author versions of three cited papers: \citet{www2020} (working paper of June 2019),
\citet{www2020b} (version of May 2020, with a correction of May 2023) and \citet{hlw2025} (version of June 2024).
The statements taken from them were checked against these versions: the definition of $I_h$, the
characterization theorem used in the representation step and the convexity theorem of \citet{www2020}; the
monotonicity criterion and Lipschitz bound, which are also stated there; the extension to general spaces, the
convention $\mathrm{ES}_1=\operatorname{ess\,sup}$ and the mixture representation of \citet{www2020b}; and the
axioms and the two characterization theorems of \citet{hlw2025}. No discrepancy was found. Lemma numbers that
could differ between these versions and the published articles were removed, and the description of
\citet{hlw2025} in Section~\ref{sec:lit} was rewritten from the paper.

The account of OpenAI's work and of the responses of the mathematical community in
Section~\ref{sec:std-practice} was assembled later, from two independent AI-prepared reports (see the Discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:std-compare}). Statements were kept where the two reports agree or where a primary source was read
directly (the GitHub repositories, the two arXiv papers and the AGMAI guidelines); OpenAI's own pages could not be
retrieved automatically; the texts of its Navier--Stokes and First Proof posts, and a localized version of its October post on academic
collaborations, were retrieved by the author in a browser, and the details of those projects are taken from them;
the October post is paraphrased rather than quoted.

\subsection{Hallucinations and semantic errors encountered}

\emph{Statements about the literature.} The main risk of hallucination concerned the exact hypotheses, conclusions
and numbering of cited results. Two entries of the digest disagreed on whether the mixing measure in the Kusuoka
representation includes an atom at the endpoint; the paper therefore states the mixture over $[0,1]$ with an
explicit convention (Corollary~\ref{cor:shape}(a)) and cites the representation without theorem numbers. For the
same reason, the representation step used in the proofs is not taken from the literature but reproved
(Lemmas~\ref{lem:cashlip}--\ref{lem:rep}).

\emph{Errors in mathematical content.} Table~\ref{tab:errors} lists the errors in AI-generated mathematics that were
found during the project. Most are semantic: the statement proved, or claimed, differed from the statement
intended, typically at a degenerate case (zero premium, the break-even set, the labels $\infty$ and $\star$) or in
the scope of a claim.

\subsection{Guardrails applied in this project}\label{sec:collab-guard}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \emph{Labels on knowledge.} Every literature statement in the digest carried a confidence label and a note on
  its source, as described above.
\item \emph{Independent summaries.} Summaries of the key cited works prepared with Gemini were compared with their
  descriptions and uses in the paper.
\item \emph{Self-contained proofs.} Results whose exact statements were uncertain are reproved; in particular the
  representation of comonotonic additive functionals, for which the proof cites \citet[Thm.~1]{www2020}, is
  reproved in Lemmas~\ref{lem:cashlip}--\ref{lem:rep}.
\item \emph{Adversarial checking.} The three checking runs on the panel's proofs were instructed to refute; one tested
  the degenerate case $\LR\equiv1$ with a premium that is not comonotonic additive, and two recomputed the numbers in
  the counterexamples. The formulation through the implied premium and risk measure (Section~\ref{sec:axioms} and Section~\ref{sec:proofs} of the Supplement) was given to a
  further checking run with instructions to look for circularity and for the cases $\Pi(X)=0$, break-even books,
  $\LR\equiv1$ and $c=0$.
\item \emph{Repair loop.} Reported gaps were returned to the prover agent; the panel's proofs went through two
  repair rounds before the final checking round.
\item \emph{Counterexamples.} Each axiom is tested against an explicit loss ratio
  (Section~\ref{sec:necessity}).
\item \emph{Recomputation.} The values in Examples~\ref{ex:PH}, \ref{ex:ES}, \ref{ex:qc} and in the counterexamples
  were recomputed by script, and the monotonicity range in Example~\ref{ex:family} was confirmed on a grid.
\item \emph{Formal checking.} The loss-ratio-specific steps were formalized in Lean and compiled; see
  Section~\ref{sec:collab-lean}.
\item \emph{A second system.} The review with ChatGPT, a model from a different provider, found three of the errors in
  Table~\ref{tab:errors} that the earlier checks had missed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the prover produced and what was formally checked}

The ``prover'' of Figure~\ref{fig:panel} is a language-model agent that writes proofs in ordinary mathematical
language; it is not a formal theorem prover. In this project it drafted the first complete if-and-only-if theorem,
in a formulation based on an assumed cash-line axiom, with proofs of the results that are now
Theorems~\ref{thm:A}, \ref{thm:B}, \ref{thm:A0}, \ref{thm:C} and~\ref{thm:Cp} (the last three in the Supplement) and of the supporting lemmas; the final
checking round of these proofs returned two reports without objections and one report of gaps in side claims, which
were repaired. The present formulation through the implied premium and risk measure
(Lemmas~\ref{lem:premium}--\ref{lem:LI} and Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}) was drafted afterwards by the main
assistant and checked by one further run, whose findings appear in Table~\ref{tab:errors}.

The loss-ratio-specific steps were also checked formally in Lean (Section~\ref{sec:collab-lean}).

\subsection{Revisions prompted by readers}\label{sec:collab-revision}

Three comments from readers of earlier versions changed the paper. They are reported here because they show where
the direction of the work came from, and because each led to further work with AI agents that the standard asks to
be recorded.

\paragraph{A public comment on the research question.}
A reader of the announcement of an earlier version questioned whether an accounting ratio deserves an axiomatic
characterization. The exchange that followed between the author and the main assistant is summarized in the
Discussion in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; it led to the motivation from risk reporting given there and changed no
mathematics.

\paragraph{Axioms on the loss ratio alone.}
In an earlier version the pooling axiom \axCM{} and the regularity axioms were stated through the implied premium
and the implied risk measure. Peng Liu, who read that version, found the characterization clean but remarked that
some axioms were therefore based on derived quantities, and that it would be preferable to state all axioms
explicitly on the loss ratio itself. The present formulation, in which every axiom refers only to values of $\LR$
(Section~\ref{sec:R}), was obtained in three steps.

\emph{Exploration by the author with the main assistant.} The author first tried to replace the implicit premium by
explicit formulas in terms of $\LR$; these attempts, and the reason they cannot suffice, are recorded in the
Discussions after Proposition~\ref{prop:explicit}. An intermediate version stated \axCM{} with weights given by the
points at which the cash lines are not finite. The author rejected it: such a point is the premium under another
name, and the requirement was that no axiom use the premium, a risk measure or a point read off a cash line.

\emph{The panel.} The author then asked for a panel to replace \axCM. It was run as a scripted workflow of nine
agents. Four proposal agents worked from different angles: stability of level sets under comonotone pooling together
with a condition on the shape of the cash lines; a single pooling identity on cash lines; elimination of the weight
from \axCM; and the limits of what a pure loss-ratio axiom can achieve. Each proposal was passed to its own
adversarial agent, instructed to refute it and to recompute its numbers. All four survived with repairs; the errors
the adversarial agents found are in the last part of Table~\ref{tab:errors}. A judge agent ranked the four, chose
the proposal that became \axPe{} and \axR, adopted the form of the regularity axioms from another proposal, and
collected the impossibility results that became Proposition~\ref{prop:nofinite} and the witnesses of
Section~\ref{sec:necessity}. The judge also checked the combined system in exact rational arithmetic on a
four-point space for three representing pairs, a pair of distortions, $h=g$ and a signed pair: \axR{} held on
every test pair (600 for each of the first and third, 450 for $h=g$), including, for the first, 75 pairs of
non-constant break-even books, 75 mixed pairs and 150 pairs whose premiums cancel; the sure amounts in \axRC{} and
\axRM{} equalled $I_g$ and $I_h$; and each witness of Section~\ref{sec:necessity} failed. The prompts of the
workflow, the judge's report and its script are kept with the records of the project (\texttt{ai\_process/}).
The main assistant then wrote Section~\ref{sec:R}, Lemmas~\ref{lem:R} and~\ref{lem:offsets} and the changes in
the other sections, and repeated the numerical checks.

\emph{The author's decisions.} The author adopted the judge's recommendation and questioned the remaining
hypothesis of \axR{} (the last Discussion in Section~\ref{sec:R}); the answer became Remark~\ref{rem:limzero} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:nofinite}. The nonnegative case (Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} of the Supplement) was left in
its earlier form. The proofs of Lemmas~\ref{lem:R} and~\ref{lem:offsets} and of Proposition~\ref{prop:nofinite}
were checked by one adversarial agent, by the judge and by computation; they are not formalized in Lean and were not
part of the review with ChatGPT.

\paragraph{The structure of the paper.}
An editor of an actuarial journal, to whom the author sent an earlier version for an opinion on the reporting
standard, suggested that the paper was long, that it was structured around the mathematical contribution with the
report on AI as a detailed acknowledgement, and that the standard should instead be a contribution in its own right,
with the mathematical result as its case study; the editor also noted that faster derivation tends to produce more
results and examples than a reader needs. The comments on the standard itself are summarized in the last Discussion
of Section~\ref{sec:std-repo}. In response, the paper was restructured with the main assistant: the standard and the
comparison with large-scale efforts now come first (Section~\ref{sec:standard}); the literature review was
shortened; the proofs, the version without law invariance, the version for nonnegative losses, the consequences of
the representation and the record of the Lean check moved to the Supplement; and the Discussions recording the
author's exchanges with the main assistant were added. The mathematical statements were not changed in this step.

\subsection{Verification status}\label{sec:collab-status}

The proofs have undergone AI-assisted critical review. Such review is fallible and does not constitute formal
verification; this applies equally to the informal review with ChatGPT. No claim of machine-checked correctness is
made beyond the results formalized in Lean, in the abstract setting and with the hypothesis described in
Section~\ref{sec:collab-lean}; those results concern the formulation with \axP, \axN{} and \axCM, and the
equivalence with the present axioms (Lemma~\ref{lem:R}) is not formalized. Independent human review of all proofs
has not yet taken place; the comments reported in Section~\ref{sec:collab-revision} concerned the formulation of
the axioms and the structure of the paper. Apart from the three papers named in Section~\ref{sec:collab-kb}, the
references have not been checked against the original publications. Section~\ref{sec:standard} and the
comparison with OpenAI's work rely on the cited public sources; where these are press reports, this is stated.

\subsection{What the case study shows about the standard}\label{sec:collab-lessons}

Four observations from the case study motivate the items of Table~\ref{tab:standard}. First, the errors were found
by different checks: checking runs within the panel, adversarial agents, the main assistant during rewriting, and a
review with a model from a different provider, which found three errors that the earlier checks had missed
(Table~\ref{tab:errors}). Reporting the checks, and not only their conclusion, tells the reader which kinds of
error have been looked for. Second, the decisive steps came from the author: the reformulation through the implied
premium and risk measure came from an objection to an AI-drafted formulation that was internally correct but
assumed what should have been proved, and the axioms on the loss ratio alone came from insisting, after a reader's
remark, that no derived quantity appear in an axiom. A record of the division of labour makes such contributions
visible. Third, the revisions after the Lean check changed the axioms, so that the formal check now covers an
equivalent earlier formulation rather than the present one; a verification status stated part by part, with dates,
makes this visible instead of hiding it. Fourth, agreement among AI reviews does not establish correctness, and a
formal check covers only what was formalized.
