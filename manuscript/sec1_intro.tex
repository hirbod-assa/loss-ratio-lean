\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

AI is now used in mathematical research in many ways. At one end, a researcher asks a chatbot for help with a
lemma and records this in a line of the acknowledgements. At the other end, organizations run large numbers of AI
agents that propose results, write proofs, attack them and, in some cases, check them in a proof assistant
(Section~\ref{sec:std-practice}). Between the two lies a mode of work that is within reach of an individual
researcher: a panel of AI agents, each with an assigned role, working on one problem under the direction of a
mathematician who poses the questions, rejects formulations and decides on content. This paper is about how results
obtained in this way should be reported.

The question is practical. A proof drafted by AI can read well and be wrong, and the errors found in the work
reported here were mostly semantic: a correct argument for a slightly different statement, typically at a
degenerate case, which careful reading can miss (Table~\ref{tab:errors}). A reader therefore needs to know not
only that AI was used, but for what, under whose direction, with which checks, which errors were found, and what
has and has not been verified. The declarations of AI use that journals ask for record much less. At the same time,
editors face a growing volume of submissions and a limited supply of reviewers, and a reporting requirement that
adds to the reviewer's burden, or that cannot be checked, will not be adopted. A standard must therefore be
proportionate, verifiable where possible, and useful to the reviewer.

The paper makes two contributions. The first is a proposed standard for reporting AI-assisted proofs in
mathematical risk management and actuarial science (Section~\ref{sec:standard}). It consists of nine items, from
the tools used to the public records of the process. The items are applied in three tiers according to how heavily
AI was used, so that a paper in which AI helped with editing is not asked for what a paper whose proofs were
developed by agents must report. Each item is marked by whether an editor or reviewer can verify it, and the
standard is designed so that reporting errors and checks honestly strengthens a paper rather than weakens it.
Formal verification in a proof assistant and a public repository are encouraged, and their scope is to be
reported precisely, but they are not required. To the author's knowledge, this is the first proposal of such a
standard in our field. It is offered for the community to test and improve.

The second contribution is a case study to which the standard is applied in full: a mathematical result obtained
with a panel of AI agents, together with the report of how it was obtained. The problem was chosen by the author as
a test. It is not a textbook exercise, its answer was not known in advance, and its solution needs machinery that is
not routine. Like most mathematicians, the author had long carried a few questions that seemed worth answering. One
of them is to characterize the loss ratio as a risk indicator by necessary and sufficient conditions, in the way
that \citet{chernymadan2009} characterized performance measures such as the Sharpe ratio and the RAROC through
their theory of acceptability indices.

The loss ratio, claims divided by premium, is among the most widely used quantities in insurance. Ex post it is
incurred claims over earned premium. Ex ante it is reported through its expected value, which can be misleading
when the risk of a book is concentrated in the tail: two books with the same expected loss ratio may differ greatly
in their exposure to large losses. A natural remedy is to report a tail measure of the claims, such as the value at
risk or the expected shortfall, divided by the premium \citep{adeh1999}. The loss ratio then becomes a model-based,
or mark-to-model, quantity: a risk measure of the loss divided by a premium principle, of which the ex-post ratio is
the special case in which the model is the realized outcome. The question is which properties are necessary and
sufficient for a functional of the loss to be a loss ratio of this kind.

\begin{discussion}[motivation of the problem]
When an earlier version was circulated, a reader asked publicly why one should characterize an accounting ratio
axiomatically at all, and whether the choice of such a problem undermined the demonstration of what AI can do. In
answering, the author and the main assistant separated the ex-post accounting ratio from the ex-ante ratio used in
risk reporting, where a tail measure over the premium is a model-based quantity whose defining properties are not
obvious. The paragraph above, and the statement that the problem was chosen deliberately as a test, are the outcome
of that exchange.
\end{discussion}

Our answer recovers the premium and the risk measure from the loss ratio itself. Along the \emph{cash line}
$c\mapsto\LR(X+c)$ of a book $X$ there is exactly one point at which the ratio is not finite; the premium is minus
this point, the sure payment at which the book changes hands, and the risk measure follows from the accounting
identity loss $=$ loss ratio $\times$ premium. With this reading, axioms that refer only to values of the loss ratio
characterize the loss ratios that are ratios $I_h/I_g$ of signed Choquet integrals (Theorems~\ref{thm:A}
and~\ref{thm:B}). The central axiom states that the underwriting results of comonotone books of zero premium add
when the books are pooled. None of the axioms can be dropped, and no axiom that depends only on finite values of the
loss ratio can replace the pooling axiom (Section~\ref{sec:necessity} and Proposition~\ref{prop:nofinite}).

The case study is reported in Section~\ref{sec:collab}, item by item against the proposed standard. Three facts
from it shaped this paper. First, the central idea of the result, the reading of the premium and the risk measure
from the loss ratio, arose from the author's objections to AI-drafted formulations that assumed what should have
been proved. Second, Peng Liu, who read an earlier version, remarked that some of its axioms were stated through the
implied premium and risk measure and asked for axioms stated more explicitly on the loss ratio itself; the present
axioms were obtained in response, with a second panel of agents (Section~\ref{sec:collab-revision}). Third, an
editor of an actuarial journal, asked for an opinion on the reporting standard, suggested that the actuarial part
be condensed and that the standard be presented as a contribution in its own right, with the mathematical result as
its case study; the paper has been restructured accordingly, and the proofs and secondary results have moved to a
Supplement.

Several passages of the paper record exchanges between the author and the main AI assistant in the course of the
work. They are set in italics and introduced by the mark $\diamond$\,\emph{Discussion}. They are formal summaries
of what was discussed and concluded, not transcripts, and they are included so that the reader can see where the
author's questions and objections changed the mathematics or the presentation.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:standard} reviews current practice, compares the approach of
this paper with large-scale multi-agent efforts, and proposes the reporting standard. Sections~\ref{sec:lit}
to~\ref{sec:necessity} present the mathematical case study: the related literature, the setting, the axioms, the
main results and the independence of the axioms. Section~\ref{sec:collab} reports the AI process of the case study
against the standard, including the formal check in Lean and the revisions. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
open problems on both sides. The Supplement contains the proofs, the version without law invariance, the version
for nonnegative losses, further consequences, and the timed record of the Lean check.
