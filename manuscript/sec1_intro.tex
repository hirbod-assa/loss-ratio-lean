\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Like most mathematicians, the author has long carried a few questions that seemed worth answering. One
of them, in actuarial science, is to characterize the loss ratio as a risk indicator by necessary and sufficient
conditions, in the way that \citet{chernymadan2009} characterized performance measures such as the Sharpe ratio
and the RAROC through their theory of acceptability indices. The loss ratio, claims divided by premium, is among
the most widely used quantities in insurance underwriting, portfolio management and reinsurance pricing. Ex post
it is incurred claims over earned premium; ex ante it is the ratio of a loss measure $\rho(X)$, such as an
expected or a risk-adjusted loss, to a premium $\Pi(X)$ of the same position $X$. When the premium is a non-linear
functional of the loss, or the numerator is a risk measure, it is not clear which functionals of a loss should be
called a loss ratio, or which properties a loss ratio should have when books are pooled.

The question arose in risk reporting. Before the claims are known, a loss ratio is usually reported through
its expected value, expected claims over premium. When the risk of a book is concentrated in the tail of its
loss distribution, the expected loss ratio can be misleading: two books with the same expected loss ratio may
differ greatly in their exposure to large losses. A natural remedy is to replace the mean of the claims by a tail
measure, and to report, for instance, the value at risk or the tail value at risk (expected shortfall) of the claims divided by the
premium \citep{adeh1999}. The loss ratio then becomes a model-based, or mark-to-model, quantity: a risk measure
of the loss, evaluated under a model of its distribution, divided by a premium principle. The ex-post accounting
ratio is the special case in which the model is the realized outcome. Once such ratios are admitted, the
question is which properties are necessary and sufficient for a functional of the loss to be a loss ratio of
this kind. The tail value at risk and the value at risk are distortion
functionals, so tail loss ratios over a distortion premium are covered by the results below
(see the ratio $\mathrm{ES}_{0.9}/\E$ in Example~\ref{ex:qc}).

This paper reports what happened when this question was put to AI. The author posed it to a panel of AI agents,
in the manner of recent multi-agent efforts to obtain mathematical results with AI: agents surveyed ten strands of
the literature, proposed candidate theorems, ranked them through judge agents, drafted proofs and
counterexamples, and submitted the proofs to repeated adversarial review, while the author set the questions,
rejected formulations and made every decision on content. The problem was chosen in part as a deliberate test.
It is not a textbook exercise, its answer was not known in advance, and its solution needs machinery that is not
routine, such as signed Choquet integrals. The paper therefore has two aims of equal weight: to answer the
mathematical question, and to show, in a form that others can inspect, how AI was used to answer it.

The second aim makes transparency a requirement. A proof drafted by AI can be fluent and wrong, and the errors
found in this project were mostly semantic: a correct argument for a slightly different statement, typically at a
degenerate case, which careful reading can miss (Table~\ref{tab:errors}). A reader therefore needs to know not
only that AI was used, but for what, under whose direction, with which checks, which errors were found, and what
has and has not been verified; the declarations of AI use that journals ask for record much less.
Section~\ref{sec:collab} gives this account for the present paper and, in Section~\ref{sec:collab-standard},
distils it into a checklist for reporting AI-assisted proofs. To the author's knowledge, this is the first attempt
to set such a standard in mathematical risk management and actuarial mathematics. It is offered as a proposal,
for the community to test and improve.

We now describe the mathematical content. We work with the \emph{extended loss ratio} $\LR=\rho/\Pi$, which allows negative values and zero premiums,
defined abstractly by \emph{association}: a family of risk measures $\{\rho_\beta\}$ is
associated with $\LR$ if $\LR(X)=\beta\iff\rho_\beta(X)=0$; the canonical family is
$\rho_\beta=(\rho-\beta\Pi)/(1-\beta)$, $\beta\ne1$. The present paper characterizes, by axioms stated on $\LR$
alone, the loss ratios that are ratios of two Choquet-type functionals.

Three features distinguish the problem from the classical characterizations of premium principles and risk
measures \citep{yaari1987,wyp1997,schmeidler1986,www2020}. First, the object is a ratio, while those results
concern a single functional. Second, the ratio does not determine its numerator and denominator: on positive
losses, $(\rho,\Pi)$ and $(\psi\rho,\psi\Pi)$ give the same ratio for every positive functional $\psi$. Third, the
ratio is not monotone: we show that a loss ratio of two distortion functionals is monotone only if it is
identically $1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:F2}). The theory of acceptability indices of \citet{chernymadan2009},
which rests on monotonicity, therefore does not apply, and the analogy that motivated the question has to be
pursued by a different route.

Our approach is to recover the premium and the risk measure from the loss ratio. For a book $X$, consider the
\emph{cash line} $c\mapsto\LR(X+c)$. A premium is the sure amount at which a risk changes hands; on the cash line,
a zero premium is a point at which the loss ratio is not finite. We define the \emph{implied premium} $\Pi(X)$ as
minus the unique such point, and the \emph{implied risk measure} by the accounting identity
$\rho(X)=\Pi(X)\LR(X)$, extended by cash shifts to books with zero premium. At such books the implied risk
measure is the residue of the cash line at its singularity (Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}). For a Bernoulli loss
$\1_A$ with $P(A)=\tfrac14$, the expected loss and the square-root distortion premium, the cash line is
$(\tfrac14+c)/(\tfrac12+c)$; it is singular at $c=-\tfrac12$, so $\Pi(\1_A)=\tfrac12$ and
$\rho(\1_A)=\tfrac12\LR(\1_A)=\tfrac14$ (Figure~\ref{fig:three}).

With this reading, Theorems~\ref{thm:A} and~\ref{thm:B} state the following. A map $\LR$ from bounded losses to
$\R\cup\{\infty,\star\}$ is of the form $I_h/I_g$, with $I_h,I_g$ signed Choquet integrals of distortions $h,g$
of bounded variation, if and only if it satisfies the following axioms, stated in Section~\ref{sec:axioms}:
\axP{} every cash line has exactly one point where $\LR$ is not finite; \axN{} $\LR(c)=1$ for sure losses
$c\ne0$; \axCM{} the loss ratio of a pool of comonotone books is a combination of their loss ratios with their relative costs, $\Pi(X)/(\Pi(X)+\Pi(Y))$, as weights; \axLI{} $\LR$ is
law invariant; and \axRC{} the implied premium and risk measure are uniformly continuous. If \axRC{} is replaced
by \axRM, monotonicity of the implied premium and risk measure, $h$ and $g$ are distortion functions and no continuity
assumption is needed. In both cases $(h,g)$ is unique, $\Pi=I_g$ and $\rho=I_h$. The unit-slope Möbius form of the
cash lines, the normalization $\Pi(c)=c$, comonotonic additivity of the implied premium, scale invariance and
the association are consequences of the axioms.

Given \axP{} and \axN, axiom \axCM{} is equivalent to comonotonic additivity of the implied premium and risk measure,
the axiom of \citet{yaari1987} and \citet{wyp1997}; once this equivalence is established, the representation step
follows from the theory of comonotonic additive functionals \citep{schmeidler1986,msm1994,cmmm2012,www2020}. The
contributions specific to loss ratios are: the construction of the implied premium and risk measure; the proof
that the pooling identity yields additivity of the implied pair on all comonotone pairs, including books that
break even, on which a premium given exogenously would not be identified (Lemma~\ref{lem:comon} and
Remark~\ref{rem:primitive}); unconditional uniqueness of $(h,g)$; the impossibility of a monotone loss ratio and
its consequences for the associated family (Corollary~\ref{cor:assoc}); a version for nonnegative losses
(Theorem~\ref{thm:C}); and counterexamples showing that none of the axioms
\axN, \axCM, \axLI, \axRC, \axRM{} can be dropped.

On the side of AI, the outcome of the experiment was as follows. The work was done with Claude (Anthropic), used
as a main assistant and to coordinate panels of AI agents on designated parts of the project: a roundtable of six
agents and a larger panel covering ten literature strands surveyed the literature, proposed candidate statements,
ranked them by judge agents, drafted proofs and submitted them to repeated AI-assisted critical review; the main
assistant constructed examples and counterexamples and drafted and revised the manuscript. ChatGPT (OpenAI) was
used for an independent informal review of the proofs, and Gemini (Google) for summaries of key cited works. The
agents produced a complete if-and-only-if theorem with proofs, but in a formulation that assumed a cash-line axiom
and so supplied $\rho$ and $\Pi$ by fiat. The formulation through the implied premium and the implied risk measure,
which is the central idea of the paper, arose from the author's objections to these AI-drafted versions. The
AI-generated material was treated as proposals throughout. The reviews found twelve errors in AI-generated
mathematics, including a wrong sufficient condition for quasi-convexity and an incorrect uniqueness claim, all of
which were corrected (Table~\ref{tab:errors}); three of them had been missed by the earlier checks and were
found by the review with a model from a different provider. The numbers in the examples were recomputed; the statements taken from three of the main
sources were checked against those papers; and the steps specific to loss ratios were formalized in the proof
assistant Lean and checked by its kernel, with the classical representation step taken as a hypothesis.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews the related literature, and
Section~\ref{sec:setting} introduces the extended loss ratio. Section~\ref{sec:axioms} defines the implied premium
and risk measure and states the axioms. Section~\ref{sec:results} contains the main theorems and examples, and
Section~\ref{sec:triviality} discusses the role of the axioms. Proofs are in Section~\ref{sec:proofs}.
Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} treats nonnegative losses, Section~\ref{sec:necessity} gives the counterexamples, and
Section~\ref{sec:consequences} derives consequences. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists open problems.
Section~\ref{sec:collab} reports the use of AI: the organization of the work, the knowledge base, the errors
found, the guardrails, the formal check in Lean (Section~\ref{sec:collab-lean}), the verification status, and
the proposed reporting standard (Section~\ref{sec:collab-standard}). Readers mainly interested in the use of AI
can read Section~\ref{sec:collab} directly after this introduction; it refers to the mathematical sections only
for the errors found and for the results checked in Lean.
