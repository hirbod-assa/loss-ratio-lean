\section{The axioms, the implied premium and the implied risk measure}\label{sec:axioms}

In this section we state the axioms and define the premium and the risk measure implied by a loss ratio. Every
axiom refers only to values of $\LR$: it evaluates $\LR$ at books and at their cash shifts, and uses only
arithmetic and whether a value is finite. No axiom refers to a premium, a risk measure, a weight, or a point
of a cash line defined from $\LR$. There are five axioms: \axPe{} and \axR{} in Section~\ref{sec:R}, \axLI{} in
Section~\ref{sec:li}, and the regularity axioms \axRC{} and \axRM{} in Section~\ref{sec:reg}. Axioms \axPe{} and
\axR{} imply three properties, \axP, \axN{} and \axCM, which are equivalent to them and which give the axioms
their meaning: a unique price point on each cash line, a loss ratio of one for sure losses, and comonotone pooling
at relative price (Proposition~\ref{prop:RPNCM}). The implied premium and the implied risk measure are minus
the price point and minus the zero of the cash line (Definition~\ref{def:impliedprem} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:zero}). They are used to interpret the axioms and in the proofs. Table~\ref{tab:axioms} at the
end of the section summarizes the axioms and the derived properties.

\subsection{Axioms \axPe{} and \axR: pooling of books with zero premium}\label{sec:R}

A premium is the sure amount at which a risk changes hands. A book whose loss ratio is not finite is one that
changes hands at zero premium: it has been transferred together with exactly its premium and commands no further
premium. We call such a book \emph{unpriced}.

\begin{axiom}[\axPe: every book can be made unpriced]\label{ax:Pex}
For every $X\in L^\infty$ there is $c\in\R$ with $\LR(X+c)\notin\R$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[\axR: comonotone pooling of unpriced books]\label{ax:R}
Let $X,Y\in L^\infty$ be comonotone with $\LR(X)\notin\R$ and $\LR(Y)\notin\R$. Then for all
$s,t,u\in\R\setminus\{0\}$ the values $\LR(X+s)$, $\LR(Y+t)$ and $\LR(X+Y+u)$ are real, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R}
 u\,\bigl(\LR(X+Y+u)-1\bigr)=s\,\bigl(\LR(X+s)-1\bigr)+t\,\bigl(\LR(Y+t)-1\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{axiom}

Axiom \axR{} is the accounting identity ``premium $\times$ (loss ratio $-1$) $=$ underwriting result'', stated in
loss ratios alone. If $X$ is unpriced, adding a sure loss $s\ne0$ creates a book whose premium is $s$, since sure
amounts are priced at face value, and $s\,(\LR(X+s)-1)$ is the underwriting result of that book, claims minus
premium, in units of cash. Axiom \axR{} says two things. First, the underwriting result does not depend on the sure
amount added, since a sure loss carries no underwriting result: this is \eqref{eq:R} with $Y=0$, once it is known
that $0$ is unpriced and $\LR(t)=1$, both of which \axR{} implies (Proposition~\ref{prop:RPNCM}). Second, the
underwriting results of comonotone books, which cannot hedge each other, add up when the books are pooled, and a
pool of unpriced books is again unpriced. The hypothesis that $X$ and $Y$ are unpriced is a statement about values
of $\LR$; it only selects the books to which the identity applies. A general book enters through its cash shifts.
Taking $u=s+t\ne0$ in \eqref{eq:R} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ravg}
 \LR(X+Y+s+t)=\frac{s}{s+t}\,\LR(X+s)+\frac{t}{s+t}\,\LR(Y+t):
\end{equation}
two comonotone books written at the premiums $s$ and $t$ pool to the average of their loss ratios weighted by these
premiums. This is pooling at relative price, with observed cash premiums as weights.

The hypothesis that $X$ and $Y$ are unpriced cannot be removed, and it cannot be replaced by a condition on finite
values of $\LR$. For a loss ratio $\LR$ write $\widetilde\LR$ for the real-valued map equal to $\LR$ where $\LR$ is
finite and to $1$ elsewhere. A condition \emph{depends on $\LR$ only through its finite values} if it holds for
$\LR$ whenever it holds for some $\LR^\circ$ with $\widetilde{\LR^\circ}=\widetilde\LR$. Conditions on limits
of finite values along cash lines, such as $\lim_{s\to0}s(\LR(X+s)-1)$ or $\lim_{|s|\to\infty}s(\LR(X+s)-1)$, are of
this kind, since under \axP{} the values $\LR(X+s)$ near the singular point are finite.

\begin{proposition}[Non-finite values are needed]\label{prop:nofinite}
Let $\Pi'=\max(I_{\sqrt t},I_{\min(1,1.5t)})$ and $\LR'=\bracket{\Pi'}{\Pi'}$. Then $\LR'$ satisfies \axPe, \axP,
\axN, \axLI, \axRC{} and \axRM, its finite values are all equal to $1$, and $\LR'$ is not of the form
$\bracket{I_h}{I_g}$ with $h,g\in\Hset_1$. Consequently, no condition that depends on $\LR$ only through its finite
values and holds for every loss ratio $\bracket{I_h}{I_g}$ can replace \axR{} in Theorems~\ref{thm:A}
and~\ref{thm:B}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Pi'$ is law invariant, monotone, $1$-Lipschitz and cash additive with $\Pi'(c)=c$, so the cash line of $X$ under
$\LR'$ equals $1$ except at $c=-\Pi'(X)$, where it is not finite. This gives \axPe, \axP, \axN{} and \axLI. The sure
amount $a$ with $\LR'(X-a)\notin\R$ is $\Pi'(X)$; a book $W$ never has $\LR'(W)=0$, and $\LR'(W)\notin\R$ with
$\LR'(W+1)=1$ holds exactly when $\Pi'(W)=0$. Both sure amounts in \axRC{} and \axRM{} are therefore $\Pi'(X)$, and
both axioms hold. If $\LR'=\bracket{I_h}{I_g}$, the two maps have the same non-finite books, so $I_g(X)=\Pi'(X)$ for
every $X$ by \axP, and $\Pi'$ would be comonotonic additive; Example~\ref{ex:indepR} shows that it is not. Finally,
$\widetilde{\LR'}\equiv1=\widetilde{\LR^\circ}$ for $\LR^\circ=\bracket{\E}{\E}$, which is of the form
$\bracket{I_h}{I_g}$; so every condition of the stated kind holds for $\LR'$. \qedhere
\end{proof}

On books that break even, the finite values of the cash line carry no information about the premium, and only the
location of the non-finite values does (Proposition~\ref{prop:explicit}). The following remark shows the same
for the limit at a vanishing premium.

\begin{remark}[The limit at zero premium]\label{rem:limzero}
For $\LR=\bracket{I_h}{I_g}$, $s(\LR(X+s)-1)=s\,(I_h(X)-I_g(X))/(I_g(X)+s)$. Its limit as $s\to0$ exists for every
$X$: it is $I_h(X)$ if $X$ is unpriced and $0$ otherwise. An axiom requiring that this limit exist is therefore
automatic, and the limit is $0$ on unpriced books with $I_h(X)=0$ as well as on all books with a premium, so it
does not identify the unpriced books. Nor can \eqref{eq:R} be stated for the limits on all comonotone pairs: take
$\rho=\E$, $\Pi=I_{\sqrt t}$, $X=\1_A$ with $P(A)=\tfrac14$ and $Y=-\tfrac12$. The limits for $X$ and $Y$ are $0$,
since $\Pi(X)=\tfrac12$ and $Y$ is a sure amount, while $X+Y$ is unpriced and its limit is $\E(X+Y)=-\tfrac14$. The
hypothesis of \axR{} accounts for such pools, whose premiums cancel.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The derived properties]\label{prop:RPNCM}
Axioms \axPe{} and \axR{} hold if and only if the properties \axP, \axN{} and \axCM{} below hold.
\end{proposition}

The proof is in Section~\ref{sec:proofs}. The rest of this section states the three properties, reads off the
implied premium and risk measure, and explains their meaning.

\subsection{The price point, the zero and the implied premium}\label{sec:implied-premium}

\begin{property}[\axP: one price point on each cash line]\label{ax:P}
For every $X\in L^\infty$ there is exactly one $c\in\R$ at which $\LR(X+c)\notin\R$.
\end{property}

\begin{definition}[Price point and zero of a cash line]\label{def:points}
Under \axP, the \emph{price point} of $X$ is the unique $\cinf X\in\R$ at which $\LR(X+\cinf X)$ is not finite.
If the cash line of $X$ has at most one zero, its \emph{zero} $\czero X$ is the unique $c\in\R$ with
$\LR(X+c)=0$ if there is one, and $\czero X:=\cinf X$ if the cash line has no zero.
\end{definition}

Both points are read off the cash line: $\cinf X$ is where the loss ratio explodes and $\czero X$ is where it
vanishes. They are the cash offsets at the levels $\beta=\infty$ and $\beta=0$, that is, the sure amounts $c$ with
$\LR(X+c)=\beta$; this is the analogue for loss ratios of the recovery $\rho_x(X)=\inf\{c:\alpha(X+c)\ge x\}$ of a
family of risk measures from an acceptability index $\alpha$ \citep{chernymadan2009}. The convention for a cash line
without a zero is explained after Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}: under the axioms, such a cash line equals $1$
away from its price point. Since the cash line of $X+c$ is the cash line of $X$ shifted by $c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pointscash}
 \cinf{X+c}=\cinf X-c,\qquad \czero{X+c}=\czero X-c\qquad\text{for all }X\in L^\infty,\ c\in\R .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Implied premium]\label{def:impliedprem}
Under \axP, the \emph{implied premium} of $X$ is $\Pi(X):=-\cinf X$, minus the price point of $X$.
\end{definition}

By \eqref{eq:pointscash},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Picash}
 \Pi(X+c)=\Pi(X)+c\qquad\text{for all }X\in L^\infty,\ c\in\R ,
\end{equation}
and $X$ belongs to the finite domain $D=\{\LR\in\R\}$ if and only if $\cinf X\neq0$, that is, $\Pi(X)\neq0$. Property \axP{} does not
distinguish between the two non-finite values; they play the role of labels (Remark~\ref{rem:labels}).

The definition formalizes the notion of a price. A premium is the sure amount at which a risk changes hands:
once the insurer holds $X$ and has received $\Pi(X)$, the position $X-\Pi(X)$ commands no further premium,
and on the cash line a zero premium is exactly a point where the loss ratio is not finite. In terms of the
canonical family $\rho_\beta=(\rho-\beta\Pi)/(1-\beta)$, for books $X$ outside the break-even set,
$\rho_\beta(X)$ is the sure offset $m$ at which $\LR(X-m)=\beta$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:assoc}(b)), and $\rho_\beta\to\Pi$ as $\beta\to\infty$; the implied premium is therefore the
member of the associated family at level $\infty$, in the same way that \citet{chernymadan2009} recover a
family of risk measures from an acceptability index $\alpha$ through $\rho_x(X)=\inf\{c:\alpha(X+c)\ge x\}$.
The definition also agrees with ratemaking practice, in which the premium is obtained from a loss cost and
a permissible loss ratio \citep{wernermodlin2016}. Finally, the price point measures the premium in units of
cash, which removes the indeterminacy $(\rho,\Pi)\mapsto(\lambda\rho,\lambda\Pi)$ of the ratio. A shift
$X+c$ with $c<0$ is the book transferred together with a sure payment $|c|$.

Property \axP{} is ordinal: it is unchanged if $\LR$ is replaced by $\psi\circ\LR$ for any $\psi:\R\to\R$, the
non-finite values being kept. It excludes ratios that are finite along a whole cash line, such as
$\arctan(\rho/\Pi)$, and ratios with several singular points.

\subsection{Property \axN: a sure loss is priced at cost}

\begin{property}[\axN]\label{ax:N}
$\LR(c)=1$ for every constant $c\neq0$.
\end{property}

A sure loss carries no risk, so its fair premium is its face value; this is the ``no unjustified loading''
property of premium principles \citep{gerber1974}. Under \axP{}, property \axN{} implies $\Pi(c)=c$ for all $c$:
the cash line of $0$ equals $1$ away from $0$, so $\Pi(0)=0$, and $\Pi(c)=c$ by \eqref{eq:Picash}. Premiums
with a proportional loading, such as $(1+\theta)\E$, violate \axN.

\subsection{Property \axCM: comonotone pooling at relative cost}\label{sec:cm}

For $X,Y\in D$ with $\cinf X+\cinf Y\ne0$ call
\[
 w_{X,Y}:=\frac{\cinf X}{\cinf X+\cinf Y},\qquad w_{Y,X}=1-w_{X,Y}=\frac{\cinf Y}{\cinf X+\cinf Y},
\]
the \emph{relative cost} of $X$ and of $Y$, respectively. Since $-\cinf X$ is the sure payment at which $X$ changes
hands, $w_{X,Y}$ is the relative price of $X$ in the comonotone portfolio $\{X,Y\}$: the price of $X$ as a fraction
of the prices of the two books together, read off their cash lines. In terms of the implied premium,
$w_{X,Y}=\Pi(X)/(\Pi(X)+\Pi(Y))$.

\begin{property}[\axCM]\label{ax:CM}
For comonotone $X,Y\in D$ with $\cinf X+\cinf Y\neq0$,
\[
 \LR(X+Y)=w_{X,Y}\,\LR(X)+w_{Y,X}\,\LR(Y).
\]
\end{property}

Comonotone books do not hedge each other, so the cost of the pooled book should be the sum of the costs of its
parts, and then $w_{X,Y}$ is the share of $X$ in the cost of the pool. This is a consequence of \axCM{} and not
part of it: it is the comonotonic additivity $\Pi(X+Y)=\Pi(X)+\Pi(Y)$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:comon}. Property \axCM{} then
says that the loss ratio of a comonotone pool is allocated to its constituents according to relative cost: the pooled
loss ratio is the combination of the two loss ratios whose weights are their relative costs. Since
$\rho=\Pi\cdot\LR$, this is the accounting statement that the loss of the pool is the sum of the losses of the
parts and the premium of the pool is the sum of their premiums, so that the loss ratio of the pool is total loss over
total premium. For books that are not comonotone, diversification will in general make the pooled premium smaller than
the sum of the premiums; this is why \citet{yaari1987} and \citet{wyp1997} impose additivity only for comonotone risks.

The weights always add up to one. They lie in $[0,1]$, and the combination is convex, exactly when $\Pi(X)$ and
$\Pi(Y)$ have the same sign, in particular when both premiums are positive, which is the case of practical interest.
Premiums of opposite sign do occur, since $\Pi(c)=c$ for sure losses and gains, and then one weight is negative and the
other exceeds one, so that the combination is affine and not convex.

A constant is comonotone with every book. Taking $Y=c$ in \axCM{} and using \axN{} and $\Pi(c)=c$ gives, for
$X\in D$, $c\ne0$ and $c\ne-\Pi(X)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pool}
 \LR(X+c)=\frac{\Pi(X)}{\Pi(X)+c}\,\LR(X)+\frac{c}{\Pi(X)+c}\cdot1
 =\frac{\Pi(X)\,\LR(X)+c}{\Pi(X)+c}.
\end{equation}
Thus along the cash line the loss ratio is the combination of the loss ratio of the book and the loss ratio $1$ of the
sure loss, with the relative costs of the two as weights: adding a sure loss pulls the ratio towards $1$, and the
singularity occurs where the relative cost of the book becomes infinite, that is, at $c=-\Pi(X)$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:readings} below shows that this makes every cash line a unit-slope Möbius map.

If $\Pi(X)$ and $\Pi(Y)$ have the same sign, then $w_{X,Y}\in[0,1]$ and \axCM{} makes $\LR(X+Y)$ a convex combination of
$\LR(X)$ and $\LR(Y)$; in particular it implies the mediant inequality: $\LR(X+Y)$ lies between $\LR(X)$ and
$\LR(Y)$. The inequality is a betweenness property, and, as in decision theory, where betweenness yields only
implicit representations while weighted averages yield ratios of linear functionals
\citep{chew1983,dekel1986}, it does not suffice for our purposes (Example~\ref{ex:cube}).

Given \axP{} and \axN, \axCM{} holds if and only if the implied premium is comonotonic additive and
$\Pi\cdot\LR$, restricted to $D$, extends to a comonotonic additive functional (Lemma~\ref{lem:comon}).

\begin{remark}[A premium given from outside]\label{rem:primitive}
Suppose that a premium functional $\Pi^\circ$ is given and \axCM{} is stated with $\Pi^\circ$ as weights. If
$\LR(X)=a\ne b=\LR(Y)$ and $\LR(X+Y)=L$, then $\Pi^\circ(X)/\Pi^\circ(Y)=(b-L)/(L-a)$, so the weights are determined
by the loss ratios. On books with $\LR=1$, however, the pooled ratio equals $1$ whatever the weights, and \axCM{}
carries no information on $\Pi^\circ$. For instance, if $\LR\equiv1$ on $\{I_g\ne0\}$, then \axCM{} holds with the
non-additive weights $\Pi^\circ=(I_g)^3$, since $a^3+b^3\ne0$ implies $a+b\ne0$. With the implied premium this
difficulty does not arise, because \axP{} locates the price point also when the ratio equals $1$ elsewhere on
the cash line.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The implied risk measure}\label{sec:implied-risk}

\begin{definition}[Implied risk measure]\label{def:impliedrisk}
Under \axP, put $\rho(X):=\Pi(X)\,\LR(X)$ for $X\in D$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:readings}
Assume \axP, \axN{} and \axCM. Then $\rho$ extends uniquely to a cash-additive functional on $L^\infty$, with
$\rho(c)=c$, and for every $X\in L^\infty$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
\item $\LR(X+c)=\dfrac{\rho(X)+c}{\Pi(X)+c}$ for every $c\neq-\Pi(X)$;
\item $\rho(X)=(\Pi(X)+c)\,\LR(X+c)-c$ for every $c\neq-\Pi(X)$;
\item $\rho(X)-\Pi(X)=\lim_{c\to-\Pi(X)}(c+\Pi(X))\,\LR(X+c)$; in particular $\rho(X)=\lim_{c\to0}c\,\LR(X+c)$
  if $\Pi(X)=0$;
\item if $\rho(X)\ne\Pi(X)$, then $-\rho(X)$ is the unique zero of the cash line of $X$; if $\rho(X)=\Pi(X)$, the
  cash line equals $1$ away from the price point.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proof is given in Section~\ref{sec:proofs}. By (c), the implied risk measure of a book with zero premium is
the residue of its cash line at the price point: if a small premium $c$ is introduced, $\LR(X+c)\approx\rho(X)/c$.
We call $N:=\{\rho=\Pi\}$ the \emph{break-even set}; $N\cap D=\{\LR=1\}$, and $N$ also contains the books with
$\rho=\Pi=0$. On $N$ the residue vanishes and, by (d), the cash line has no zero, so that the risk measure cannot
be read from the zero of the cash line; this is why it is defined through (b).

\begin{corollary}[The implied risk measure is minus the zero]\label{cor:zero}
Assume \axP, \axN{} and \axCM. Then every cash line has at most one zero, and $\rho(X)=-\czero X$ for every
$X\in L^\infty$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}(d), if $\rho(X)\ne\Pi(X)$ the cash line of $X$ has the unique zero $-\rho(X)$. If
$\rho(X)=\Pi(X)$, it equals $1$ away from the price point and has no zero, so $\czero X=\cinf X=-\Pi(X)=-\rho(X)$.
\qedhere
\end{proof}

Thus the implied risk measure, like the implied premium, is a point of the cash line: $\rho(X)$ is minus the sure
amount at which the loss ratio of $X$ vanishes, and on the break-even set the convention of
Definition~\ref{def:points} makes the two points coincide.

Away from the break-even set, the premium can be computed explicitly from two values of the loss ratio. Solving
\eqref{eq:pool} for $\Pi(X)$ gives the following formulas.

\begin{proposition}[Explicit formulas]\label{prop:explicit}
Assume \axP, \axN{} and \axCM, and let $X\in D$ with $\LR(X)\ne1$. Write $\phi_X(c)=\LR(X+c)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),nosep]
\item \emph{Two quotes.} For every $c\notin\{0,-\Pi(X)\}$, $\phi_X(c)\ne\LR(X)$ and
  \[
   \Pi(X)=\frac{c\,\bigl(1-\phi_X(c)\bigr)}{\phi_X(c)-\LR(X)},\qquad
   \rho(X)=\LR(X)\,\frac{c\,\bigl(1-\phi_X(c)\bigr)}{\phi_X(c)-\LR(X)} .
  \]
\item \emph{Sensitivity to a sure loss.} $\phi_X$ is differentiable at $0$, $\phi_X'(0)=(1-\LR(X))/\Pi(X)\ne0$, and
  \[
   \Pi(X)=\frac{1-\LR(X)}{\phi_X'(0)},\qquad \rho(X)=\frac{\LR(X)\,\bigl(1-\LR(X)\bigr)}{\phi_X'(0)} .
  \]
\item \emph{Underwriting result.} $\Pi(X)-\rho(X)=\lim_{|c|\to\infty}c\,\bigl(1-\phi_X(c)\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\LR(X)=1$, then $\phi_X\equiv1$ away from the price point, and $\Pi(X)$ is not determined by the finite values
of the cash line.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}(a), $\phi_X(c)=(\rho+c)/(\Pi+c)$ with $\rho=\rho(X)$, $\Pi=\Pi(X)\ne0$, so
\[
 1-\phi_X(c)=\frac{\Pi-\rho}{\Pi+c},\qquad \phi_X(c)-\LR(X)=\frac{c\,(\Pi-\rho)}{\Pi\,(\Pi+c)} .
\]
Since $\LR(X)\ne1$ means $\rho\ne\Pi$, the second expression is non-zero for $c\ne0$, and the quotient gives (a).
Differentiating, $\phi_X'(c)=(\Pi-\rho)/(\Pi+c)^2$, so $\phi_X'(0)=(1-\LR(X))/\Pi$, which gives (b); and
$c\,(1-\phi_X(c))=c\,(\Pi-\rho)/(\Pi+c)\to\Pi-\rho$, which gives (c). If $\LR(X)=1$, then $\rho=\Pi$ and
$\phi_X(c)=1$ for $c\ne-\Pi$. \qedhere
\end{proof}

Formula (b) reads: the implied premium is the underwriting margin $1-\LR(X)$ divided by the sensitivity of the
loss ratio to a sure loss; formula (a) is its finite-difference version, which needs only two quoted loss ratios,
with and without a sure loss $c$. For $X=\1_A$ in Example~\ref{ex:three}, $\LR(X)=\tfrac12$ and
$\phi_X(1)=\tfrac56$, so (a) gives $\Pi(X)=\tfrac16/\tfrac13=\tfrac12$. For books outside $D$ the formulas apply to
$X+c$ with $X+c\in D$, and $\Pi(X)=\Pi(X+c)-c$ by \eqref{eq:Picash}.

The last statement of Proposition~\ref{prop:explicit} is the reason why the axioms must refer to non-finite
values of $\LR$, as \axPe{} and \axR{} do. On the break-even set the cash line carries no information about the premium except the
location of its singularity, and Remark~\ref{rem:primitive} shows that the weights in \axCM{} are then not
identified by the values of the ratio. Off the break-even set, the weights of \axCM{} could equally be written with
the explicit premium of (a) or (b) in place of $-\cinf{}$; but \axCM{} must also govern pools that contain
break-even books, and there only the price point locates the premium. Axiom \axR{} avoids weights altogether: it
moves each book to zero premium by a sure payment, which is a statement about non-finite values, and pools there.

\begin{remark}[A weight-free form of \axCM]\label{rem:weightfree}
Property \axCM{} is a cardinal statement whose weights are read off the price points. Given \axP, the pair
\axN+\axCM{} is equivalent to the following two statements, which use no weights:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),nosep]
\item[(M)] every cash line has at most one zero, and $\LR(X+c)=\dfrac{c-\czero X}{c-\cinf X}$ for all $c\ne\cinf X$;
\item[(A)] if $X,Y$ are comonotone, then $\cinf{X+Y}=\cinf X+\cinf Y$ and $\czero{X+Y}=\czero X+\czero Y$.
\end{enumerate}
(M) says that every cash line is the unit-slope Möbius map determined by its own zero and price point; (A) says
that comonotone pooling adds the cash offsets at the levels $0$ and $\infty$. Indeed, \axN{} and \axCM{} imply (M) by
Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}(a) and Corollary~\ref{cor:zero}, and (A) by Lemma~\ref{lem:comon}(a). Conversely,
(A) with $X=Y=0$ gives $\cinf0=\czero0=0$, so (M) gives $\LR(c)=c/c=1$ for $c\ne0$, which is \axN. If $X,Y\in D$ are
comonotone with $\cinf X+\cinf Y\ne0$, then $X+Y\in D$ by (A), and (M) at $c=0$ gives
$\LR(Z)=\czero Z/\cinf Z$ for $Z\in\{X,Y,X+Y\}$; by (A),
$\LR(X+Y)=(\czero X+\czero Y)/(\cinf X+\cinf Y)=w_{X,Y}\LR(X)+w_{Y,X}\LR(Y)$, which is \axCM. Unlike the formulation
(CP) of Remark~\ref{rem:oldCP}, (M) refers to no functional other than $\LR$, but it still uses the points
$\cinf X$ and $\czero X$. Axiom \axR{} states the content of (M) and (A) without them.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Three cash lines]\label{ex:three}
Let $\rho=I_{\mathrm{id}}=\E$ and $\Pi=I_{\sqrt{\cdot}}$, and let $P(A)=\tfrac14$. Figure~\ref{fig:three} shows
the cash lines of three books.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
\item $X=\1_A$: $\LR(X+c)=(\tfrac14+c)/(\tfrac12+c)$. The price point is $-\tfrac12$, so $\Pi(X)=\tfrac12$, and
  $\rho(X)=\Pi(X)\LR(X)=\tfrac14$; the cash line vanishes at $-\tfrac14$.
\item $Y=\1_A-\tfrac12$, a book with zero premium: $\LR(Y)=\infty$ and $\LR(Y+c)=(c-\tfrac14)/c$. The residue at
  the price point $0$ is $-\tfrac14=\rho(Y)$.
\item $Z\equiv\tfrac12$, a break-even book: $\LR(Z+c)=1$ for $c\ne-\tfrac12$, and $\LR(Z-\tfrac12)=\star$. The
  price point gives $\Pi(Z)=\tfrac12$; the cash line has no zero, and $\rho(Z)=\Pi(Z)\cdot1=\tfrac12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Labels at the price point]\label{rem:labels}
To have $\LR=\bracket{\rho}{\Pi}$ also at price points, one may adopt the convention
\begin{center}
\ax{S}\quad at the price point, $\LR=\star$ if the residue in (c) is zero and $\LR=\infty$ otherwise.
\end{center}
Convention \ax{S} is not used in the proofs. If, instead, the loss ratio is real valued with $0/0:=1$, the price
point of a break-even book cannot be observed and the implied premium is not defined there.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A formulation by cash lines]\label{rem:oldCP}
One may alternatively postulate (CP): every cash line is a unit-slope Möbius map,
$\LR(X+c)=\bracket{\rho(X)+c}{\Pi(X)+c}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:readings}(a) shows that (CP) follows from \axP,
\axN{} and \axCM, the labels agreeing under \ax{S}. Conversely, (CP) implies \axP{} and \ax{S}, and (CP) together
with \axCM{} implies \axN: if $\pi_0:=\Pi(0)\ne0$, \axCM{} applied to the constants $a=b=-\pi_0/2$ forces
$\LR(-\pi_0)\in\R$, although $-\pi_0$ is the price point of $0$; hence $\pi_0=0$, and \axCM{} with $X=Y=a\ne0$ gives
$(\rho(0)+2a)/(2a)=(\rho(0)+a)/a$, i.e.\ $\rho(0)=0$ and $\LR(c)=1$. Thus \axP+\axN+\axCM+\ax{S} is equivalent to
(CP)+\axCM.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Axiom \axLI: law invariance}\label{sec:li}

\begin{axiom}[\axLI]\label{ax:LI}
If $X\eqd Y$, then either $\LR(X)=\LR(Y)$ or both values are non-finite.
\end{axiom}

This is the standard assumption of premium and risk-measure theory \citep{wyp1997,kusuoka2001}. Because the
implied premium and risk measure are computed from cash lines, \axLI{} passes to them (Lemma~\ref{lem:LI}).
Ratios that weight scenarios non-uniformly, such as $\E_Q/\E$ with $Q\neq P$, violate \axLI; they are covered by
Theorem~\ref{thm:A0}.

\subsection{Axioms \axRC{} and \axRM: regularity of the cash offsets}\label{sec:reg}

The regularity axioms concern two sure amounts attached to a book: the amount $a$ whose payment leaves the book
unpriced, $\LR(X-a)\notin\R$, and the amount $a$ whose payment leaves it without loss. A book $W$ has no loss if
$\LR(W)=0$, or, when $W$ is unpriced, if $\LR(W+1)=1$, that is, if $W$ written at the premium $1$ costs exactly
that premium.

\begin{axiom}[\axRC]\label{ax:RC}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that for all $X,Y\in L^\infty$ with $\|X-Y\|_\infty<\delta$ and
all $a,b\in\R$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
\item if $\LR(X-a)\notin\R$ and $\LR(Y-b)\notin\R$, then $|a-b|<\varepsilon$;
\item if each $W\in\{X-a,\,Y-b\}$ satisfies [$\LR(W)=0$, or $\LR(W)\notin\R$ and $\LR(W+1)=1$], then
  $|a-b|<\varepsilon$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[\axRM]\label{ax:RM}
Let $X\le Y$ almost surely and $a,b\in\R$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
\item If $\LR(X-a)\notin\R$ and $\LR(Y-b)\notin\R$, then $a\le b$.
\item If each $W\in\{X-a,\,Y-b\}$ satisfies [$\LR(W)=0$, or $\LR(W)\notin\R$ and $\LR(W+1)=1$], then $a\le b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{axiom}

Taking $X=Y$ shows that each axiom forces the two amounts to be unique, so the axioms make sense independently of
the others. Under \axPe{} and \axR, the amount in (i) is the implied premium $\Pi(X)$ and the amount in (ii) is the
implied risk measure $\rho(X)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:offsets}); thus \axRC{} (respectively \axRM) says exactly that $\Pi$ and $\rho$
are uniformly continuous (monotone) on $L^\infty$, and \axRM{} implies \axRC{} (Lemma~\ref{lem:cashlip}). In
terms of the cash lines, the two amounts are minus the price point and minus the zero. Axiom \axRM{} says that a
larger loss must be offset by a larger sure amount, both to be priced at zero (level $\infty$) and to have no loss
(level $0$). This is the monotonicity of the cash offsets familiar from acceptability indices
\citep{chernymadan2009}, imposed at the two levels that determine the cash line; \axRC{} is the corresponding
continuity. For a ratio $\bracket{A}{B}$ with $A,B$ cash additive, the two amounts are $B(X)$ and $A(X)$, so that
the axioms reduce to continuity or monotonicity of $A$ and $B$. We include the signed tier \axRC{} because the
canonical family $\rho_\beta$ is a difference of monotone functionals and is in general not monotone
(Corollary~\ref{cor:assoc} and Example~\ref{ex:family}). Monotonicity is imposed on the cash offsets and not on the
values of the ratio, for the following reason.

\begin{proposition}[No monotone loss ratio]\label{prop:F2}
Let $h,g\in\Hset_1$ with $g>0$ on $(0,1]$, and let $\LR=I_h/I_g$ on $\Xp:=L^\infty_+\setminus\{0\}$.
If $\LR$ is monotone on $\Xp$ (non-decreasing or non-increasing for the almost sure order),
then $h=g$ and $\LR\equiv1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(A)=p>0$. For $c\ge0$, $\LR(\1_A+c)=\frac{h(p)+c}{g(p)+c}$ is monotone in $c$ with derivative at
$0$ of the sign of $g(p)-h(p)$. Next let $P(E)=q>0$ and $t\ge0$. Then $X_t=1+t\1_E\in\Xp$ is
increasing in $t$, and $I_k(X_t)=1+t\,k(q)$ for $k\in\{h,g\}$, so
$\LR(X_t)=\frac{1+t\,h(q)}{1+t\,g(q)}$ has derivative at $t=0$ of the sign of $h(q)-g(q)$. If $\LR$
is non-decreasing both signs are non-negative, hence $h=g$ on $(0,1]$; if $\LR$ is non-increasing both are
non-positive, and again $h=g$. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:F2}
Let $h=\mathrm{id}$, $g=\sqrt{\cdot}$ and $p=q=\tfrac14$ in the proof. Both families of books in
Figure~\ref{fig:F2} increase in the almost sure order, but the loss ratio increases along the first and
decreases along the second: adding a sure loss to an over-priced book moves the ratio towards $1$ from below,
whereas adding risk to a sure loss moves it away from $1$.
\end{example}

Consequently, the theorems on acceptability indices \citep{chernymadan2009}, which require monotonicity of the
index, do not apply to loss ratios. Axiom \axRM{} is nevertheless an order property of $\LR$: it concerns the
sure amounts at which the ratio becomes non-finite or vanishes, not the size of the ratio.
